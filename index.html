Digital Literacy & Computer Science Education

The digital world is changing fast. I'm making sure students are ready to lead it.

Every day, students are shaped by technology they were never taught to understand: the apps they scroll, the tools that answer their questions, the systems that decide what they see. I don't think the answer is to ban it or to simply hand it over. The answer is literacy.

I'm a computer science educator, and after 15 years in classrooms I believe every student deserves to know how these systems work, who they serve, and who they might leave out. When students understand that, they stop being users of the digital world and start becoming the people who question it and shape what comes next.

My Why

Digital systems run our lives. They shape what we see, how we learn, how we work, and how we vote. Most people have never been taught how they actually work.

I believe that has to change. Everyone deserves to understand how computer systems work, not just the people who build them. Digital literacy isn't a bonus skill for a lucky few. It's what lets us be informed voters and helps us make our world better. Right now, who gets that understanding too often depends on their school, their district, or their job.

That's why I do this work. I've spent my career building programs, training teachers, and designing curriculum so every student can cross the stage career-ready and able to lead in a digital world they truly understand. The technical expertise matters, but relationships are what move people. That has always been my real superpower.

Curriculum & Pathway Design

A four-course pathway that turns students into systems thinkers

The Software & Systems Development Pathway at San Diego Unified asks one question in each course. Together, they move students from understanding systems to owning their role in them.

  1. CS Discoveries: What are computer systems?
  2. AP CS Principles How have computer systems impacted the world?
  3. AP CS A How can I build computer systems?
  4. CS 3-4 What is my role in software and systems development?

I designed the pathway to align with the CTE Model Curriculum and CSTA standards, and I wanted every student who completed it to leave with something real: at least one professional certification, a digital portfolio they're proud of, and genuine work-based learning experience. I built in equitable grading practices and intentionally designed inclusive spaces that increased participation from female students, students with IEPs, and students from historically underrepresented communities.

Pathway completers leave with industry-recognized credentials, a professional digital portfolio, and hands-on work-based learning experience.

AI Literacy & Industry Alignment

An AI-assisted agile framework that makes assessment AI-proof

I partnered with ICT industry professionals to audit my curriculum and redesign it around how modern teams actually work. Out of that came something I'm especially proud of: an AI-assisted agile framework, the same short-cycle way software teams plan, build, and improve their work. Students set goals in short work cycles, document their thinking, test with real users, gather feedback, and reflect along the way. Because it assesses the process, not the product, it's AI-proof by design.

I've also had advice and mentorship from industry representatives at Apple, Google, Microsoft, and PlayStation on digital literacy and curriculum, and I bring what I learn back to my classroom.

Students experience the full design cycle of planning, building, testing, and reflecting, using the same collaborative tools and practices they'll meet in the workforce.

Teaching & Leadership Philosophy

How I think about digital literacy in the age of AI

Literacy now, not ban vs. allow.

The debate over whether to ban or allow AI tools misses the point. Every student, family, and community member needs to understand the systems shaping their lives, whether or not their school, district, or job has a policy on AI. Waiting to see what happens is a choice too, and the students who pay for it are the ones who can least afford to.

Literacy is for everyone. Tool fluency is for careers.

Digital and AI literacy is a civic necessity, and it belongs to every student. Tool fluency is different. It's career-specific, for students in CTE pathways who need to meet industry practice. When we treat literacy as optional or advanced, we recreate the same gaps we've always had with newer technology. The students who need it most are the ones we can least afford to leave behind.

Assess the process, not the product.

In the age of AI, the product proves nothing while the thinking behind it proves everything. Process-based learning is AI-proof by design: when students plan, document, test, reflect, and iterate, we see their minds at work in ways no generated output can fake. This isn't just good assessment practice. It's the foundation of how real teams actually build things.

Behind every system are people.

If AI can write code, students can spend more time on what matters most: who a system serves, who it might harm, and what it costs. That means privacy, mental wellness, bias, and the environment. It means asking who owns your face and voice, and whether you ever agreed to their use. It also means noticing when students start treating AI like a friend, because that deserves our attention.

Educators can't lead students into a future they haven't entered themselves.

We cannot keep teaching from the sideline of a transformation that is already happening in our classrooms. Leading in the age of AI requires a special type of vulnerability: the willingness to learn alongside students, rebuild what instruction looks like, and collaborate with colleagues rather than wait for a directive from above. The educators who will move their schools forward are the ones brave enough to go first.
